# Script: Why does the world synchronize time zones to Greenwich?
**Niche**: History & Culture
**Duration**: 3 minutes (approx. 470 words)
**Format**: Horizontal 16:9
**Language**: English

## Global direction (applies to every scene below, revision after QC feedback)
- **Hook discipline**: first 3-10s must open on the paradox/conflict, not a neutral scene-setup. Scene 1 rewritten below to lead with the "tiny town rules the planet" paradox and an early flash of the Britain-France clash, instead of opening on a calm travel montage.
- **Subtitles**: kinetic, word/2-3-word chunks appearing in sync with the voiceover, not the whole line dumped at once. Keywords (GREENWICH, 72%, FRANCE, NEUTRAL, numbers, place names, years) get an accent highlight color (yellow/orange). No static white rectangle box, no forced ALL CAPS — let type render free-floating over the art with enough stroke/shadow contrast to stay readable, so it doesn't block the graphics underneath.
- **Motion pacing**: no scene should coast on a plain Ken Burns pan for more than ~2 consecutive beats. Any beat centered on a map coordinate (Azores, Bering Strait) or a data reveal (percentages, vote tally) gets a global zoom-out to globe scale, then a hard zoom-in onto the specific point, instead of a generic drift.
- **SFX**: whoosh alone is not enough for a 2D flat-vector video, SFX carries roughly half of the perceived smoothness. Add pop/click for icons, numbers, flags, and stamps appearing; a gavel strike at the moment of the 1884 vote; rising clock-ticking under France's multi-decade refusal to concede.

## Scene 1: Introduction (revised hook, opens on paradox + conflict, not travel montage)
- **Time**: 00:00 - 00:35
- **Visual**: Global zoom-out from space, hard zoom-in onto a tiny dot near London (Greenwich) while the rest of the globe's time-zone grid pulses in sync, selling the paradox in the first 3 seconds. Immediately after, a quick clash cut: British and French flags snapping toward each other with a red vs. blue tug-of-war arrow at center screen (the conflict teased before it's explained). Then pull back to the plane-hop map (London to Paris to New York) as a lighter beat, ending on a zoom into the small town of Greenwich with the observatory, and the three paper stamps (Eiffel Tower, Capitol Building, blank ocean with a question mark).
- **SFX**: Sub-bass whoosh on the space zoom-in, sharp flag-snap pop on the Britain/France clash cut, jet whoosh + fast ticking clock over the travel montage, "ding" pop when each paper stamp appears.
- **Audio (Narration)**:
  - **vo_01**: Why does a tiny town near London dictate the exact time for the entire planet?
  - **vo_02**: Every clock, every flight schedule, every stock exchange on Earth still bends to one meridian line.
  - **vo_03**: And for decades, France refused to accept it.
  - **vo_04**: Resetting your watch when you land in a new country feels routine today.
  - **vo_05**: But in the 19th century, this same idea sparked a fight between empires.
  - **vo_06**: So why Greenwich, and not glamorous Paris, or a neutral spot in the middle of the ocean?

---

## Scene 2: The Chaos of Local Time
- **Time**: 00:35 - 01:05
- **Visual**: A timeline slider scrolling back to the 1800s. Two adjacent towns are drawn, each with a sun directly overhead, but one town's clock reads 12:00 while the other reads 12:05. A map of the US appears, fragmented into hundreds of colorful, chaotic pieces with different time digits. A steam train runs jerkily with a spinning clock above it.
- **SFX**: Steam train whistle blowing, chaotic clock ticking that suddenly stops.
- **Audio (Narration)**:
  - **vo_07**: The answer takes us back to the 19th century.
  - **vo_08**: At that time, the concept of standard time didn’t exist at all.
  - **vo_09**: Every city and town set its own local time based on the sun's position.
  - **vo_10**: When the sun was directly overhead, that was 12 noon.
  - **vo_11**: As a result, the United States had hundreds of different time zones,
  - **vo_12**: and train travel in Britain was a chronological nightmare.

---

## Scene 3: The International Meridian Conference
- **Time**: 01:05 - 01:20
- **Visual**: A large title card appears: "Washington D.C. - October 1884". Behind it is a neoclassical domed hall illustration. Flags of different nations rotate around a large round table, and arrows representing conflicting opinions clash at the center.
- **SFX**: The chairperson's gavel bang sounds authoritative and dignified.
- **Audio (Narration)**:
  - **vo_13**: To solve this chaos, in October 1884,
  - **vo_14**: the International Meridian Conference was held in Washington D.C.
  - **vo_15**: Representatives from 25 nations debated fiercely to choose a single prime meridian.

---

## Scene 4: The French Opposition
- **Time**: 01:20 - 01:45
- **Visual**: Vector 2D portrait of astronomer Jules Janssen, with a speech bubble showing a Greenwich observatory crossed out in red. A balance scale of justice appears, with "British Empire" on one side and "French Empire" on the other, tipping unstably. The globe rotates; when the Azores and Bering Strait are named, camera zooms OUT to full-globe scale first, then hard zooms IN onto that exact coordinate with a solitary blue dashed line marking it (not a generic drift pan). Next to it is a meter ruler measuring the globe.
- **SFX**: Pages flipping rapidly, gentle warning chime (ding ding), soft whoosh on the globe zoom-out/zoom-in pair.
- **Audio (Narration)**:
  - **vo_16**: The French, led by astronomer Jules Janssen, vehemently opposed choosing Greenwich.
  - **vo_17**: They argued that the prime meridian had to be a completely neutral line,
  - **vo_18**: not belonging to any global power.
  - **vo_19**: They proposed placing it in the Azores or the Bering Strait,
  - **vo_20**: just like how the metric system was created based on the size of the Earth, rather than a national landmark.

---

## Scene 5: The Anglo-American Pragmatic Argument
- **Time**: 01:45 - 02:05
- **Visual**: Split screen, one side showing British and American representatives pointing at a giant shipping ledger. A pie chart pops up with a glowing 72% region labeled "Greenwich charts" and a darker 28% region. A large merchant ship loaded with cargo glides across the screen under the shadow of Greenwich. Piles of gold coins stack up, accompanied by a downward-pointing warning arrow.
- **SFX**: Coins clinking, deep warm ship horn blowing.
- **Audio (Narration)**:
  - **vo_21**: But the British and the Americans had a much more pragmatic argument:
  - **vo_22**: 72% of the world's shipping commerce at the time was already using charts based on the Greenwich meridian.
  - **vo_23**: Starting over from scratch with a new location would cause a massive economic loss.

---

## Scene 6: The Vote
- **Time**: 02:05 - 02:22
- **Visual**: The voting scoreboard appears like a classic retro electronic display: "Greenwich Meridian Vote: Yes: 22". The tally ("22") counts up in a rapid punchy burst timed to the voiceover cadence, not a slow reveal. Next, a single Dominican Republic flag snaps in with a bright red "No: 1". Then French and Brazilian flags grey out, labeled "Abstain", each landing on its own quick beat rather than fading in together.
- **SFX**: Gavel strike on "Finally, the vote took place", rapid pop/click per country flag or number landing, alarm buzzer when "No" appears, murmur of a bustling crowd underneath.
- **Audio (Narration)**:
  - **vo_24**: Finally, the vote took place.
  - **vo_25**: 22 countries voted in favor of Greenwich.
  - **vo_26**: Only a single country voted against: San Domingo, now the Dominican Republic.
  - **vo_27**: Two countries abstained: Brazil and, of course, France.

---

## Scene 7: France's Resistance
- **Time**: 02:22 - 02:40
- **Visual**: A calendar flipping rapidly from 1884 to 1911. Paris clock and London clock showing a time offset. A vintage French law page appears with large handwriting highlighted in yellow: "Paris Time - 9m 21s". A cartoonish French character shrugs with a witty smile.
- **SFX**: Clock ticking rising in tempo under the calendar flip (1884 to 1911, France refusing to concede), pages flipping sound, playful French horn playing a teasing tune, ticking cuts out sharply on the shrug beat.
- **Audio (Narration)**:
  - **vo_28**: France refused to accept this defeat for decades.
  - **vo_29**: They continued to use Paris time until 1911.
  - **vo_30**: Even when they conceded, French law called the new time 'Paris mean time, retarded by 9 minutes and 21 seconds'
  - **vo_31**: rather than ever calling it Greenwich time.

---

## Scene 8: Conclusion
- **Time**: 02:40 - 03:00
- **Visual**: The Greenwich Prime Meridian metal strip glows on the ground at the Royal Observatory under a simulated flycam zoom-out. Ship icons, industrial gears, and the globe blend harmoniously. The entire world map turns into a neat grid of time zones, sparkling under a starry sky.
- **SFX**: Long, deep synth pad swelling for a satisfying resolution, wind chimes ringing softly.
- **Audio (Narration)**:
  - **vo_32**: Today, the Greenwich Meridian is not just a metal strip on the ground at the Royal Observatory.
  - **vo_33**: It is proof that sometimes, practical convenience and economic power shape how we measure the world,
  - **vo_34**: rising above all political disputes.
